The hardest part of raising a puppy isn't the chewed shoes — it's the eight hours you're not home. Between potty accidents, separation anxiety, and a puppy who treats your sofa like a chew toy, the workday can feel like a constant crisis. The fix isn't more time; it's a schedule that uses the time you have deliberately.

A realistic plan beats a perfect one every time. Here's a full-day structure that works for owners who leave the house for work, built around what puppies actually need at each stage.

## The First Rule: Crate Time Is Not Punishment

Puppies need 18 to 20 hours of sleep a day, and they don't know how to settle on their own. A crate gives your puppy a den — and gives you a break without guilt. The catch is that the crate has to earn its reputation. Feed meals inside it, toss treats in while you walk past, and never use it as punishment.

<ArticleImage src="/images/puppy-schedule-working-owners/2.jpg" alt="A puppy sleeping in a dog crate" variant="wide" aspect="16/9" credit="Photo: Pexels" />

<Callout type="tip" title="Build the habit first">
Practice short crate sessions while you're home: five minutes, then ten, with a stuffed toy inside. If the crate is a calm, boring, positive place before your first workday, being left alone in it is far less scary.
</Callout>

## A Day Built Around Potty Breaks

A rough rule: a puppy can hold it for about one hour per month of age, plus one — and much less when excited. An 8-week-old puppy needs a potty break roughly every two hours during the day, which usually means a midday visit from you, a dog walker, or a trusted neighbor. Plan for it before the first accident, not after.

<ImageText src="/images/puppy-schedule-working-owners/5.jpg" alt="A dog walker walking a puppy in a park" side="left" aspect="4/3">
The midday visit isn't optional luxury; it's the difference between a dog who learns to hold it and one who learns that the rug is a bathroom. Fifteen minutes of potty plus a short walk resets the whole afternoon.
</ImageText>

<Steps title="A realistic workday">
<Step>6:30 — potty, breakfast, 15 minutes of play or a short walk.</Step>
<Step>7:30 — crate with a stuffed toy; leave for work.</Step>
<Step>12:30 — dog walker visit: potty, short walk, and a food puzzle.</Step>
<Step>17:30 — potty, dinner, then 10 minutes of training.</Step>
<Step>21:30 — last potty, then lights out in the crate.</Step>
</Steps>

<FactCard title="Potty math for planning">
<Fact label="8 weeks old" value="Potty roughly every 2 hours" />
<Fact label="12 weeks" value="Potty every 3–4 hours" />
<Fact label="16 weeks" value="Most puppies manage a workday with one visit" />
</FactCard>

## Enrichment for the Hours You're Away

The toys left in the crate matter as much as the walker. A frozen stuffed toy, a puzzle feeder, or a rotating set of chew toys turns alone time into active problem-solving instead of lonely pacing. A tired mind beats a tired body: mental work settles puppies faster than extra exercise, and it's far easier to fit into a lunch break.

<ImageText src="/images/puppy-schedule-working-owners/3.jpg" alt="A puppy chewing on a rubber chew toy" side="right" aspect="4/3">
Rotate two or three enrichment toys so each one feels new. A frozen stuffed toy bought a few days of calm for many owners — just supervise the first use and remove anything that cracks or shreds.
</ImageText>

<Callout type="warning" title="Chew safety">
Never leave a puppy alone with a toy you haven't tested. Check stuffed toys for loose pieces and replace chews that develop cracks or sharp edges.
</Callout>

## The Evening Reset That Makes It Stick

Evenings are when the routine either locks in or falls apart. Keep the sequence the same every night: a walk, dinner, a short training session, then quiet time. A predictable bedtime beats a long play session — sleep is when puppies actually grow, process the day, and build the calm that makes tomorrow easier.

<ArticleImage src="/images/puppy-schedule-working-owners/4.jpg" alt="A person playing with a puppy in a backyard" variant="spotlight" aspect="16/9" caption="Dinner-time play is the day's last big energy burn — keep it short and fun." credit="Photo: Pexels" />

<PullQuote cite="A rule of thumb">
A predictable bedtime beats a long play session. Consistency is the real training tool.
</PullQuote>

The workday never gets shorter, but the puppy does get older — and every week, the schedule needs a little less babysitting. Stick with the structure, and by four months you'll have a dog who naps through your absence and saves the chaos for the evening, exactly when you're home to enjoy it.
